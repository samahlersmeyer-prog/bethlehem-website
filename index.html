    Communion at Bethlehem

    At Bethlehem, we believe that in Holy Communion Jesus truly comes to us with His body and blood, given in, with, and under the bread and wine for the forgiveness of sins and the strengthening of faith. This meal is one of God’s gifts to His people, bringing us Christ’s grace and drawing us together in unity.

    As the Small Catechism teaches, those who receive this gift are called to examine themselves: recognizing their sin, trusting in Jesus for forgiveness, believing that Christ is truly present in this meal, and desiring to live in faith and love.

    Because we believe Communion expresses a shared confession of faith, Bethlehem practices communion in fellowship with the teachings of the Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod. If you are not communing today or would like to learn more, you are warmly invited to come forward for a blessing by crossing your arms over your chest, or speak with our pastor after the service.

    What we believe

    Saved by grace, through faith, in Christ alone

    As a congregation of the Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod, we confess the faith taught in Holy Scripture and summarized in the Lutheran Confessions. Here is the heart of those teachings.

    1. Scripture

      2 Timothy 3:15–16

      The Bible is God's inspired Word, written by holy men and without error.

    2. The Triune God

      Matthew 3:16–17

      There is one true God: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, three distinct Persons in one divine being.

    3. God the Father

      Genesis 1:1

      The Father Almighty created heaven and earth and all creatures, and He loves us as His children.

    4. Jesus Christ

      John 1:1–2

      Jesus is true God, begotten of the Father from eternity, and true man, born of the Virgin Mary. He is our Savior and Lord.

    5. The Holy Spirit

      Titus 3:4–8

      The Spirit creates faith through the Gospel and Baptism, and keeps us in faith through the Lord's Supper and God's Word.

    6. Sin

      Romans 8:1–4

      Every person is born sinful and separated from God, and we sin in thought, word and deed.

    7. Salvation in Christ

      John 3:16

      Christ fulfilled the Law in our place, died for our sins and rose again. Salvation comes only through Him, never through our own works.

    8. Faith

      Ephesians 2:8–9

      Salvation is God's free gift, received by faith. Each person is called to confess Jesus as Savior.

    9. Law and Gospel

      Romans 3:23–24

      The Law shows how God wants us to live and where we fall short. The Gospel proclaims our salvation in Jesus Christ.

    10. Forgiveness and mission

      Matthew 28:18–20

      God daily and richly forgives our sins by grace, for Christ's sake. The church's mission is to bring this Gospel to all people.

    Mission, vision & values

    A catalyst for Gospel transformation in southeast Fort Wayne

    Our vision is a neighborhood where all people experience Christ's love, grow in faith, and restore hope together. Our mission has three parts.

    Proclaiming Christ

    We center everything on Jesus, grounding ourselves in biblical truth and the historic Christian faith. Knowing people encounter the gospel in unique ways, we proclaim Christ clearly and live out His love, mercy, and justice with faithfulness to Scripture and sensitivity to our neighbors. Our worship, teaching, and community life bear witness to His grace in word and deed.

    Nurturing Faith

    We nurture faith as a shared journey where people of all backgrounds and generations encounter God's transforming grace. Through relationships shaped by mutual honor and discipleship across all ages, we pass on faith, learn from one another, and grow together as God's family. We celebrate the richness of His kingdom, where the gospel restores dignity, unites us across every divide, and calls us into a new identity in Christ.

    Restoring Hope

    We are called to be Christ's presence in our neighborhood, particularly southeast Fort Wayne, recognizing the dignity and strength already present in our community. Believing that God's light shines in all circumstances, we engage in partnerships that foster mutual transformation and lasting flourishing.

    Our values

    • We are Christ-CenteredJesus is at the heart of everything we do. His grace shapes our worship, relationships, and mission.
    • We Stand on God's WordWe are rooted in the historic Christian faith, committed to the authority of Scripture as God's infallible Word that guides our beliefs, decisions, and practices in an ever-changing world.
    • We Embody the GospelWe proclaim and live out the gospel, demonstrating Christ's love, mercy, and justice so that all may know His grace, restoration, and belonging.
    • We Celebrate God's Diverse KingdomWe rejoice that the gospel unites all people in Christ. We seek to reflect this diversity in our life together and actively pursue unity and reconciliation in our community.
    • We Nurture Faith Across All AgesThe church is a family where faith is passed from one generation to the next. We value deep relationships across all ages, nurturing discipleship in both young and old.
    • We Have Hope for Our NeighborhoodTrusting that Christ is already at work in southeast Fort Wayne, we walk alongside and work with our neighbors, partnering for mutual flourishing and lasting hope.
    • We Accomplish More Together Than AloneWe recognize that the work of the kingdom is bigger than us. With humility and joy, we collaborate with others who share our heart for gospel-centered community engagement.
    • We are Generous with God's GiftsWe faithfully steward the gifts God has entrusted to us, our time, resources, and building, so that they may be used for His kingdom and the flourishing of others.

    Our story · 1926–2026

    Throughout all generations

    “Lord, You have been our dwelling place throughout all generations.” Psalm 90:1. The story of Bethlehem Lutheran Church and School, compiled and edited by Donna Volmerding for our centennial.

    The theme for Bethlehem Lutheran Church’s centennial celebration is “Lord, You have been our dwelling place throughout all generations” (Psalm 90:1).

    The mission of Bethlehem Lutheran Church is “Proclaiming Christ. Nurturing Faith. Restoring Hope.”

    Bethlehem is a place where Christ is born anew in the hearts of all who enter, and a house of bread — that is the meaning of Bethlehem — where souls are nourished with the word of God.

    “Now to Him Who is able to do immeasurably more than all we ask or imagine, according to His power that is at work within us, to Him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus throughout all generations, for ever and ever! Amen.” Ephesians 3:20-21

    Chapter One

    The seed of faith (1925-26)

    The story of Bethlehem Lutheran Church, 3705 S. Anthony Blvd. in Fort Wayne, Indiana, begins not with grand plans or ambitious visions, but with the simple needs of faithful families seeking to nurture their children in God’s word.

    These families, who lived in the rapidly growing southeast area of Fort Wayne, were separated by miles of buggy rides and later automobile travel from their home churches, making regular attendance and Christian education increasingly difficult for their children.

    With hearts full of hope and hands ready for work, these devoted members approached their respective congregations with a humble request: permission to open a branch school in their neighborhood. The response was one of Christian love and support.

    On August 27, 1925, members of two established Fort Wayne congregations held a special meeting at Concordia Lutheran Church on South Anthony Boulevard (now Shepherd of the City Lutheran Church). At this meeting, with the Rev. H. C. Luehr of Zion Lutheran Church, 2313 S. Hanna St., serving as chairman, and G. M. Schmidt, a member of Concordia Lutheran Church’s school faculty, serving as secretary, members decided to open a branch school of Zion and Concordia congregations on the southeast side of Fort Wayne. Schmidt was appointed instructor of the school, located in a portable chapel at 3205 Alexander St. The school formally opened September 13, 1925.

    This simple structure, weathered by Indiana winters and warmed by summer suns, would become the cornerstone of a century-long legacy. The portable building served its purpose well through that first winter, providing Christian education for the children of the southeast Fort Wayne community.

    Chapter Two

    A congregation is born (1926-29)

    For the convenience of those members who lived in the neighborhood area, regular Sunday morning worship services were held in the portable chapel beginning in January 1926. The services were in the charge of Concordia and Zion pastors and were assisted by professors from Concordia College (now Concordia Theological Seminary). What had begun as an educational outreach was transforming into something greater — a spiritual home for families hungry for fellowship and the word of God.

    As spring turned to summer in 1926, the small but growing congregation that gathered each Sunday began to envision their future. The convenience of local worship had blossomed into something deeper — a sense of community, of shared mission, of divine calling.

    In July 1926, faithful church members took a bold step of faith: they petitioned their mother congregations to organize a completely new congregation in southeast Fort Wayne. The request was met with blessing and support, and members obtained the necessary authorization from both Zion and Concordia Lutheran churches.

    On September 14, 1926, under a crisp autumn sky, the new congregation was formally organized, with the Rev. H. D. Mensing as interim pastor. The Rev. M. E. Reinke was Bethlehem’s first called pastor and was installed in August 1927.

    Husbands, fathers, sons, brothers and friends, with their wives and families, stepped forward to sign the constitution, forever enshrining their names as charter members of Bethlehem Lutheran Church.

    These signatures represent individual commitments as well as entire families who chose to plant their spiritual roots in new soil. Each name carried with it the hopes and dreams of families and future generations who would call Bethlehem their spiritual home. The choice of the church’s name, Bethlehem, spoke to the congregation’s understanding of their mission: to be a place where Christ is born anew in the hearts of all who enter, and a house of bread — that is the meaning of Bethlehem — where souls are nourished with the word of God.

    The young congregation wasted no time in establishing its Lutheran identity. In March 1927, less than six months after its formal organization, the new church applied for membership in The Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod. This application represented more than bureaucratic formality; it was a declaration of theological commitment, a pledge to uphold the ancient truths of Scripture and the Lutheran Confessions that had guided God’s people for centuries.

    During these formative years, the congregation continued to grow, and the portable building on Alexander Street felt the comfortable strain of success. The first infant, Maxine Macke, was baptized in September 1926, and the Bethlehem Ladies Society was organized in December of the same year. By the end of 1926, there were 125 confirmed members at Bethlehem. The first burial, Betty Jean Grote, came a little over a year later in January 1928. The first marriage to be performed was in April 1929, that of Leonard Bearman and Myrtle Harris.

    Children’s voices filled the weekday schoolroom, while on Sundays the same space echoed with hymns of praise and the proclamation of God’s word. It was clear that God was blessing this venture of faith, and the congregation began to explore a more permanent accommodation.

    Chapter Three

    Building the foundation (1926-40)

    As the 1920s ended, Bethlehem Church was at a crossroads. The congregation had outgrown its humble beginnings, and the call to expand was clear. In 1929, with the confidence that comes from witnessing God’s faithfulness, preliminary plans were drawn for a new building that would serve both church and school functions. The congregation set its sights on a promising piece of property on Anthony Boulevard, a location that would serve them well for generations to come.

    The timing seemed challenging — in 1929, the stock market crashed, bringing economic uncertainty to the nation — but the congregation’s faith remained steadfast. On September 15, 1929, the Fort Wayne landscape painted in brilliant autumn colors, the cornerstone of the new school building was laid with great ceremony and thanksgiving. This stone, carrying within it the hopes and prayers for the congregation, would witness nearly a century of Christian education and worship.

    The text included in the dedication history book of 1940 stands out as timeless words of praise and thanksgiving. “The past has been written. We know not what the future holds in store for our congregation. We only know that God’s mercies in Christ Jesus will not fail us. As we recall our many failings and shortcomings, our lack of appreciation of the priceless blessings which have been ours, our indifference and our neglects, we cry out, ‘God be merciful to us and forgive us.’”

    The congregation’s patience and perseverance through the construction process was rewarded on January 26, 1930, when the new building was dedicated with great joy. The structure was designed with versatility in mind, serving the dual purpose of educating children during the week and gathering the congregation for worship on Sundays. This shared space reflected the Lutheran understanding that education and worship are inseparable components of Christian life, that the same God Who calls us to praise Him also calls us to grow in wisdom and knowledge.

    Throughout the 1930s, Bethlehem continued to flourish even as the Great Depression tested the faith and resources of communities across America. The congregation’s commitment to Christian education remained unwavering, and families sacrificed to ensure their children could receive instruction in God’s word with their secular studies. The church became not just a place of worship, but a beacon of hope and stability in uncertain times.

    By 1939, it was clear that God had blessed the congregation beyond its humble beginnings. The growing membership had outgrown even its newer facility, and plans for a dedicated church building began to take shape. The decision to build a separate sanctuary reflected the congregation’s desire to create a space devoted entirely to worship and the celebration of God’s grace.

    On November 5, 1939, another cornerstone was laid, this time for a beautiful new church building that would serve as Bethlehem’s spiritual heart. The ceremony was filled with thanksgiving for the past and anticipation for the future. Less than a year later, on August 4, 1940, the congregation gathered for the dedication of its new sanctuary, a moment that represented the fulfillment of dreams nurtured over fifteen years of faithful service.

    Chapter Four

    Growth and service (1940-60)

    The dedication of the new church building in 1940 marked the beginning of what would become two decades of remarkable growth and expanding ministry. As World War II raged across the globe, Bethlehem Church became a fortress of faith for its members, providing spiritual strength and community support during one of history’s most challenging periods.

    The church’s commitment to serving others was exemplified in several ways during the war years. Members supported the war effort through prayer, service and sacrifice, while the congregation maintained its focus on nurturing faith in the next generation. The day school continued to thrive, providing moral and spiritual formation, as well as academic instruction, that would guide students throughout their lives.

    By 1940, the congregation had grown to impressive numbers: 1,034 souls called Bethlehem home, with 670 confirmed members actively participating in the life of the church. The day school enrolled 182 children, while the Sunday School welcomed 191 students each week. These numbers represented more than statistics — they reflected families, individuals and children whose lives were shaped by the gospel message proclaimed from Bethlehem’s pulpit and taught in its classrooms.

    The post-war period brought new opportunities and challenges. In 1947, ground was broken for an additional education building, reflecting the congregation’s continued commitment to Christian education. This expansion demonstrated remarkable foresight, as enrollment continued to climb throughout the following decade.

    The 1950s marked a golden age of growth for Bethlehem. The final major addition to the educational complex was completed and dedicated in September 1955, adding five new classrooms, a modern kitchen, a spacious cafeteria and a gymnasium. This comprehensive facility represented the congregation’s holistic approach to Christian formation — nurturing not just minds and souls, but bodies through physical education and hot lunches.

    The wisdom of this expansion became clear just three years later when, in 1958, school enrollment reached an astounding 693 students. This achievement earned Bethlehem School a distinctive honor: it became the largest elementary school in the entire Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod. This remarkable accomplishment reflected not just the church’s commitment to education, but also the community’s trust in Bethlehem’s ability to provide excellent academic instruction grounded in Christian values.

    The Rev. Arno C. Scholz, who served Bethlehem Church as senior pastor from 1931-65, wrote this brief history of Bethlehem in 1967:

    “On the occasion of the 25th anniversary of the congregation, we wrote, ‘The history of our congregation proclaims the goodness of our Lord and the marvels of His faithfulness and forbearance.’

    “That must still be our conviction today, 16 years later. There have been many changes in the course of the 41 years since our church was founded. Many of the charter members have fallen asleep in Jesus and are awaiting the great resurrection morn; the shepherd’s staff has changed hands several times; the first portable chapel, which served both as school and church, has given way to a large complex of buildings valued in excess of one million dollars. And there have also been great changes in our community.

    “But one thing has not changed. The same message is still proclaimed from the pulpit and in the classroom — the message of full and free forgiveness through the atoning death of our Lord Jesus Christ. Down through the years, sinners have been called upon to repent, to accept Jesus as their Savior and Redeemer, and to commit themselves with all that they are and have to Him.

    “True to God’s promise, this message has borne fruit. The small group of dedicated and faithful men and women who organized Bethlehem in September 1926 has grown into one of the largest congregations in The Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod. While the greatest growth took place between 1940 and 1950, there has been continued growth even in these last years. Soli Deo Gloria!

    “What the future holds in store for us as a congregation, God only knows. We do know that tremendous changes are taking place in the world, in the nation and in the church. Conditions all about us are such that all thoughtful Christians must be aware of the fact that the end of all things and our Lord’s return for the final judgment cannot be far off. This truth ought to inspire us to ever greater efforts in the work of our Christian calling while it is day, before the night comes when no man can work. Soon the things of this earth will be no more. Only what is done for Christ is of eternal value. To that let us rededicate ourselves every morning.”

    Soli Deo Gloria

    The Rev. Arno C. Scholz passed away December 1, 1967

    Chapter Five

    Worship and witness (1960-80)

    “Make a joyful noise to the Lord, all the earth.”

    The 1960s brought new dimensions to Bethlehem’s worship life, beginning with a significant enhancement to the church’s music ministry. In 1965, three magnificent new bells were installed and dedicated to the glory of God, bells that would ring out across the community, proclaiming the presence of God in our lives, calling the faithful to worship and marking the sacred moments of congregational life — baptisms, confirmations, weddings and services of remembrance.

    These three bells are comprised of red copper and tin: one weighs approximately one ton, one weighs a half-ton and one weighs a quarter ton. They were cast in the Netherlands, Denmark and Cincinnati. Each bell is inscribed with the words “In memory of those who faithfully heeded the call to worship and now worship before the throne of the Lamb.”

    The following year, 1966, had another transformative addition to Bethlehem’s worship experience. On March 27, a beautiful new pipe organ was dedicated and placed into service. Described as the “queen of musical instruments,” this organ would provide decades of glorious accompaniment to congregational singing and serve as a vehicle for praising God and exalting the Lord of the Church. The installation of the pipe organ reflected the congregation’s understanding that worship deserves our finest offerings and that beautiful music has the power to lift hearts and souls toward heaven.

    Throughout the 1960s and ‘70s, Bethlehem Church and School continued to play a vital role in the spiritual life of Fort Wayne. The congregation maintained its strong commitment to Lutheran doctrine while adapting to changing times and growing community needs.

    The church’s influence extended far beyond its walls through the thousands of students who received a quality education in Bethlehem’s classrooms, carrying Christian values into their adult lives and future careers.

    Chapter Six

    Challenges and transitions (1980-2010)

    The final decades of the 20th century and the beginning of the 21st century brought both challenges and opportunities to Bethlehem Lutheran Church. As with many established congregations, Bethlehem faced the realities of changing demographics, evolving educational needs and shifting community patterns that would require adaptation and renewal.

    Throughout the 1980s and ‘90s, the congregation continued its ministry of worship, education and service, even as Fort Wayne’s educational landscape began to change. The growth of public-school options, the rise of homeschooling and changing family structures all impacted enrollment patterns at Lutheran schools across the nation.

    The congregation’s heart for service found new expression in 1980 with the establishment of a food bank that operated in conjunction with the Associated Churches of Allen County.

    This ministry exemplifies Bethlehem’s understanding that Christian faith must be expressed through concrete acts of love and service, providing both food for the body and opportunities for spiritual conversations. The food bank is a tangible expression of Christ’s love that provides assistance to families in need while offering opportunities for church members to serve their neighbors, and it continues to serve as a beacon of hope and practical assistance for families facing economic hardship.

    The social and cultural changes of the late 20th century and a changing neighborhood compelled Bethlehem’s congregation to balance traditional Lutheran values with contemporary ministry methods, maintain its theological moorings and ensure that the timeless gospel message remains relevant to each new generation of believers.

    A significant transition occurred in 2009 when Bethlehem Lutheran School, with Zion Lutheran Academy and Unity Lutheran School, merged to form Lutheran South Unity School, located at 5401 S. Calhoun. Four churches — Bethlehem, Zion, Peace and Mount Calvary Lutheran churches — involved in this consolidation represented both the end of an era and the beginning of a new chapter in Lutheran education in Fort Wayne.

    The merger was a bittersweet moment for the Bethlehem congregation. For more than eight decades, the school had been an integral part of the church’s identity and mission. Generations of families had chosen Bethlehem precisely because of the excellent Christian education available on-site. The school had been a source of pride, a tool for evangelism and a means of serving the broader community.

    However, the merger also represented wisdom and stewardship. By joining forces with other Lutheran schools, the combined institution could offer enhanced programs, better facilities and increased stability for Lutheran education in the region. The decision reflected the congregation’s ability to adapt to changing circumstances while maintaining its commitment to Christian education.

    From the archives: the 2009 Lutheran South Association announcement

    Momentous Lutheran South Association unanimously approved

    March 24, 2009

    After decades of merger talks between southside Lutheran churches and schools, groundbreaking history has been made. At four separate voters assemblies at Bethlehem Lutheran Church, Peace Lutheran Church and Zion Lutheran Church on March 15, and Mount Calvary Lutheran Church on March 22, congregations unanimously passed a resolution to join in a new Lutheran South Association. This means that the face of Lutheran education on the south side of Fort Wayne will be changing for the better.

    “I am excited about this new cooperative venture,” says the Rev. Douglas Punke of Zion Church. “It’s really momentous in Lutheran education in Fort Wayne.”

    The reasons for the merger include situations that have been plaguing Lutheran education for years: declining enrollment and financial struggles. Instead of succumbing to pressures, however, the congregations took solid steps toward not only maintaining Lutheran education on the south side of Fort Wayne, but with dramatic plans to improve it and increase enrollment.

    “It’s not totally new to any of us,” says the Rev. Jeff Geisler, senior pastor at Bethlehem. “The struggles and financial woes we’ve all gone through are just temptations for us to let go, but with God’s love, strength and wisdom, we will make our school and churches stronger than ever.”

    The merger process this time around began last fall with the pastors of the four congregations meeting informally to brainstorm ideas and discuss options for merging the three schools: Bethlehem, Zion Academy and Unity. It became clear rather quickly that the time for merging had finally come.

    In December, a steering committee was formed comprised of leadership from all four congregations and all three schools. In addition, the Indiana District and executives from Concordia Lutheran High School provided leadership and assistance. Talks progressed quickly.

    The Lutheran Foundation learned of the plans and approached the committee offering to pay for True North Strategic Advisors to assist the steering committee beginning in January. As a result of the discussions and planning, a definition document was produced and approved by the steering committee for presentation to congregations for an official vote. The definition document outlines basic agreements for governance, finances, curriculum, facilities, staff and enrollment/marketing.

    The steering committee asserted a list of “shared values,” forming the basis of agreement and goals of Lutheran South Association:

    • Vibrant Christian education in Lutheran context
    • Significant, life-changing impact on students and their families
    • Commitment to diversity
    • Growing enrollment
    • Implementation of leading-edge teaching methodologies
    • Strong community presence on the south side of Fort Wayne
    • Cooperation among Lutheran congregations
    • Affordable education of a superior quality on the south side of Fort Wayne

    An informational open house was held in March for members of the four churches and three schools. At that presentation, members and parents were given the outline of the plan in the form of a PowerPoint presentation. This presentation and the entire definition document are available online at Peace’s website — www.PeaceLutheranFW.org.

    Transition plans are well under way already, following quickly on the heels of congregational approvals.

    “Transition can be difficult, but it is not insurmountable,” says the Rev. Donald Porter of Mount Calvary Church. “It requires people to be open to adjusting their habits and preconceptions to something different.”

    The new school board, comprised of three members of each of the four congregations, was elected and held its inaugural meeting on March 23, 2009, the day after the last congregation passed the plan. The new board is now in the process of translating the spirit of cooperation and the outline of the agreement into details of a working school.

    Staffing is expected to be in place already by April 10.

    The working name for the school will temporarily be Lutheran South Unity School until the new school board makes a final determination. Unity was chosen as the primary site mainly because of its neutral and centralized location, since the South Calhoun campus is not onsite for any of the four churches.

    The venture will not only allow four congregations to provide students with a high-quality Lutheran education, but other benefits are substantial. Each congregation will realize significant savings to their budgets, with a collective savings of nearly $450,000. By pooling resources and managing an effective cost-per-student ratio, each congregation can explore new opportunities for other ministries. Each school will bring new curriculum ideas to the table and a richness and depth of faculty for collaboration. The school will be collectively stronger with greater resources for students and expanded opportunity for outreach to the community at the school and congregational levels.

    A new curriculum committee with be meeting this month to plan changes that will emphasize maintaining accreditation and offering to students teaching techniques that mirror the best practices available in education today.

    A facility committee is readying the building for an influx of students.

    A governance committee is drafting new policy-based board guidelines.

    The Rev. Paul Spira of Peace Church is equally excited.

    “From the very first meeting, we recognized that God had presented us with a tremendous opportunity to create something new and exciting, and the steering committee has worked hard to craft a new school organization that is both innovative in its thinking and true to our Lutheran heritage. Our God is good!”

    Chapter Seven

    Renewed mission and vision (2010-26)

    The consolidation of the school did not mark an ending but a new beginning for Bethlehem Church. Freed from the demands of operating a large educational institution, the congregation was able to focus more intensively on other aspects of ministry and mission. This period is characterized by renewal, refocus and recommitment to the gospel message that has always been at the heart of Bethlehem’s identity.

    The congregation’s mission statement, “Proclaiming Christ. Nurturing Faith. Restoring Hope,” encapsulates the theological foundation that has guided Bethlehem throughout its century of ministry. This three-fold mission reflects the Lutheran understanding of the gospel’s power — proclaiming Christ brings salvation, nurturing faith through the Word and Sacrament brings growth, and restoring hope through God’s promises brings healing to broken lives. Each element builds upon the others, creating a comprehensive approach to Christian ministry that has characterized Bethlehem’s service throughout all generations.

    On Bethlehem’s 85th anniversary, it was written, “As we recall the many abundant blessings our Father in heaven has so richly showered upon our congregation, we are humbly grateful and from our hearts there rises the cry, ‘Not unto us, O Lord, not unto us, but unto Thy Name give glory.’ We face the future confident of our Father’s unfailing presence.”

    In recent years, Bethlehem has embraced new forms of ministry and outreach, utilizing modern technology and contemporary methods to share the precious gospel message. The congregation has maintained its commitment to traditional Lutheran worship while exploring ways to make the service more accessible and meaningful to visitors and new members.

    The church building on South Anthony Boulevard continues to serve as a landmark in Fort Wayne, its steeple pointing heavenward as a reminder of eternal truths in a rapidly changing world. The bells installed in 1965 still ring out across the neighborhood, and the pipe organ dedicated in 1966 continues to lead congregational singing and provide beautiful accompaniment to worship.

    Community service remains a hallmark of Bethlehem’s ministry, with members actively engaged in various forms of outreach and assistance. The congregation continues to support its food bank, participate in community events, support many mission projects and provide assistance to families in need. These activities reflect the Lutheran understanding that faith without works is dead, and that genuine Christianity must express itself through love and service to others.

    Chapter Eight

    Lessons from a century

    As Bethlehem Lutheran Church celebrates one hundred years of ministry, it’s worth reflecting on the lessons learned and the constants that have remained throughout this remarkable journey. Several themes emerge from this century of faithful service:

    Proclaiming Christ. From the very beginning, Bethlehem has maintained an unwavering commitment to proclaiming the gospel of Jesus Christ. Whether through Sunday sermons, school chapel services or individual conversations, the congregation has consistently exalted Christ as Lord and Savior.

    Nurturing Faith. The congregation’s dedication to Christian education, from the original branch school to the decades of excellence that followed, demonstrates a deep understanding that faith must be carefully tended and developed throughout all generations.

    Restoring Hope. Through times of economic hardship, a world war, global upheaval, personal tragedies and community challenges, Bethlehem has served as a place where hope is restored through the promises of God’s word and the fellowship of believers.

    The Centrality Of Worship. Through all the changes and challenges, Bethlehem has maintained its focus on the centrality of worship in the life of the Christian community. The investment in quality music, beautiful facilities and meaningful liturgy reflects the congregation’s understanding that worship is both a privilege and a responsibility.

    The Call To Service. From the early days of supporting families during the Great Depression to the establishment of the food bank in 1980, Bethlehem has consistently understood that Christian faith must express itself through service to others. This commitment to practical Christianity has been a hallmark of the congregation’s witness.

    Chapter Nine

    Throughout all generations

    The theme verse for Bethlehem Church’s centennial celebration, “Lord, You have been our dwelling place throughout all generations” (Psalm 90:1), perfectly captures the essence of the congregation’s century-long journey. One of the most remarkable aspects of Bethlehem’s history is how God has used this spiritual dwelling place to shelter, nurture and sustain families across multiple generations.

    Throughout all generations, Bethlehem has served as a spiritual dwelling place in which God’s people have found refuge, strength and community. The psalmist’s words remind us that, while earthly structures may change and human leadership may transition, the Lord remains constant as our dwelling place from generation to generation. This truth has been lived out in the life of Bethlehem Lutheran Church, where families who signed the charter in 1926 have seen their children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren baptized, confirmed, married and eventually laid to rest through the ministry of this congregation. These family connections create a living tapestry of faith that spans the entire century.

    Thousands of infants and others have been baptized at the baptismal font into the family of God, with each baptism representing new life and new hope for the future. Confirmation classes have prepared countless young people to take their place as adult members of the congregation, equipped with the knowledge of Scripture and the Lutheran Confessions to guide them throughout their lives.

    The altar has been the site of hundreds of weddings, joining couples in holy matrimony and establishing new families that contribute to the ongoing life of the congregation. These ceremonies represent personal celebrations and community commitments that support and nurture these new unions.

    The sanctuary has offered comfort during loss, with funeral services honoring members and giving grieving families hope of resurrection. The continuity of these sacred rituals across multiple generations creates a sense of stability and permanence in an ever-changing world.

    Chapter Ten

    Looking forward

    As Bethlehem celebrates its centennial, the congregation is engaged in both opportunities and challenges in the years ahead. The landscape of American Christianity continues to evolve, with shifting demographics, changing cultural attitudes toward faith, and new technologies that create both obstacles and opportunities for gospel ministry.

    The congregation’s location in Fort Wayne positions it well to serve a diverse and growing community. The city’s economic development and population growth provide opportunities for outreach and evangelism, while the established Lutheran presence in the area offers possibilities for cooperation and mutual support among congregations.

    The challenge moving forward will be to maintain the theological integrity and commitment to excellence that have characterized Bethlehem’s first century while adapting methods and approaches to reach new generations of potential members. This will require wisdom, creativity and, above all, reliance on the Holy Spirit’s guidance.

    The congregation’s commitment to serving others will undoubtedly continue to be a central aspect of its ministry. Whether through food banks, community service projects or individual acts of kindness, Bethlehem’s members will continue to demonstrate Christ’s love through practical service.

    A Legacy Of Faith

    As Bethlehem Lutheran Church celebrates its centennial, its history reflects steady service and dedication to the gospel of Jesus Christ. From the 107 charter members who signed the constitution in 1926 to the current congregation celebrating in 2026, thousands of individuals and families have found a spiritual home, Christian education and community support within these walls.

    The portable building on Alexander Street is long gone, replaced by the beautiful facilities on South Anthony Boulevard. The original charter members have been joined by generations of new believers, each adding their own chapter to the ongoing story of God’s faithfulness. The challenges have been many — economic hardship, war, social change and institutional transitions — but through it all, the congregation has remained anchored to the unchanging truths of Scripture and the Lutheran Confessions.

    Perhaps most significantly, Bethlehem’s century of ministry demonstrates the power of ordinary people committed to extraordinary faithfulness. The founders who established a branch school in 1926 could never have imagined that their humble beginning would lead to decades of ministry that touched thousands of lives. The members who sacrificed to build new facilities during the Depression could not have foreseen the generations of children who would receive Christian education in those classrooms. The congregation that installed bells and an organ in the ‘60s could not have predicted how those instruments would continue to call people to worship and praise God into the 21st century.

    This is the miracle of the church — that God takes our modest offerings of time, talent and treasure and multiplies them far beyond our ability to imagine. The seeds planted in southeast Fort Wayne in 1925 have produced a harvest of faith that continues to grow and spread throughout the community and beyond.

    As Bethlehem Lutheran Church celebrates one hundred years of serving as God’s dwelling place throughout all generations, the congregation looks forward with the same faith that sustained its founders. The mission of proclaiming Christ, nurturing faith and restoring hope continues to guide the church’s vision for the future, confident that the Lord Who has been their dwelling place for the past century will continue to shelter and sustain them for generations yet to come. The same God Who provided for a portable building on Alexander Street continues to provide for every need of His people, calling them to faith, hope and service today.

    The story of Bethlehem Church is ultimately not about buildings, programs or institutional success — though all of these have played important roles. It is about people — people created in God’s image, redeemed by Christ’s sacrifice and called to share the hope of the gospel with a world in need of good news.

    As the congregation celebrates one hundred years of faithful ministry, it does so with the confidence that the best chapters of its story are yet to be written. The God Who has been faithful for the past century will continue to be faithful for the century to come. The gospel that has sustained and motivated Bethlehem through its first hundred years will continue to transform lives and communities for generations yet to come.

    To God be the glory, great things He has done. To God be the glory for the century past, and to God be the glory for the century to come.

    Looking ahead

    The next 100 years

    For a century, God has gathered His people on Anthony Boulevard around His Word and Sacraments. Bethlehem began as a neighborhood church, and by His grace it still is one. Our food bank has served hundreds of families every year since 1980, and the neighborhood knows Bethlehem as a place to find help.

    As we begin our second century, our calling has not changed: proclaiming Christ, nurturing faith and restoring hope on Fort Wayne’s southeast side. Our building has room to grow, with a gym, a kitchen, classrooms and more, and we pray that God will fill it with new life, new neighbors and new ways to serve.

    “Lord, You have been our dwelling place throughout all generations” (Psalm 90:1). The One who has been faithful for 100 years will be faithful still. Come and be part of the next chapter.
